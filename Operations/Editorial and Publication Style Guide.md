# Kenya in Data — Editorial and Publication Style Guide

## Editorial position

Kenya in Data publishes research-led explanations for an informed general audience. The writing should carry the authority of a policy report and the clarity of high-quality data journalism.

The target register is:

- **Formality:** high, but not ceremonial.
- **Technical precision:** high.
- **Jargon:** low; necessary specialist terms are defined at first use and in a glossary.
- **Conversational language:** low in the report body.
- **Interpretation:** direct, neutral and proportional to the evidence.
- **Reading level:** an interested non-specialist should understand the main argument without prior economics training.

## Reference models

Kenya in Data borrows different strengths from four publication traditions:

1. **Institute for Fiscal Studies:** disciplined policy-report structure, concise key findings and clear separation between evidence and judgement. See the IFS report [*The context for the March 2024 Budget*](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/context-march-2024-budget).
2. **Our World in Data:** chart-led explanation, visible provenance, reusable data and a clear account of definitions and limitations. See [OWID's research article structure](https://ourworldindata.org/measure-working-hours) and its approach to [highlighting data sources](https://ourworldindata.org/redesigning-our-interactive-data-visualizations).
3. **ONS and the UK Government Analysis Function:** plain language, an inverted-pyramid structure, descriptive headings, integrated charts and transparent uncertainty. See [*Writing about data*](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-data/).
4. **World Bank economic updates:** locally grounded economic reporting, formal presentation of fiscal evidence and a clear progression from headline conditions to detailed analysis. See the [Kenya Economic Update](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099112125062524216/pdf/P507067-4f692fc7-8081-4630-9dd2-d3768ce9defc.pdf).

These are reference models, not voices to imitate. Kenya in Data should remain recognisably its own publication.

## Standard article structure

Use this order unless the subject clearly requires a different one:

1. **Finding-led title:** state the subject and analytical focus without sensationalism.
2. **One-sentence standfirst:** explain what the publication covers and why it matters.
3. **Key findings:** three to six complete statements containing the essential numbers and reporting dates.
4. **Scope and reporting dates:** identify the sources, observation periods, coverage and any important denominator rules.
5. **Analytical sections:** arrange findings from most important to most detailed. Each section should follow the sequence below.
6. **Interpretation and limitations:** consolidate caveats that affect how the evidence may be used.
7. **Conclusion:** restate the evidence and its significance without introducing a new claim.
8. **Glossary:** define specialist terms in plain language.
9. **References:** provide formal citations and direct links to primary sources.
10. **Data and project materials:** link readers to reusable files, figures, methods and source documentation.

Within each analytical section, use this sequence:

> **Finding → evidence → figure or table → interpretation → limitation, if material**

## Voice and tone

### Use

- Neutral institutional language: “The Treasury reported…” or “The data indicate…”
- Active verbs and short sentences.
- Finding-led headings: “Domestic debt carried a higher average interest rate.”
- Exact dates, units and denominators.
- Plain explanations immediately after technical terms.
- Calibrated language: “indicates”, “is consistent with”, “does not establish”.
- Author–date citations placed close to the claim they support.

### Avoid

- Dialogue with the reader or editorial team: “Do we know…?”, “we need to…”, “this sounds like…”.
- Process narration: “The cleanest way is…”, “we decided to…”, “this article will…”.
- Question headings when a supported finding can be stated directly.
- Unexplained acronyms and technical labels.
- Academic throat-clearing, long literature-review passages or inflated terminology.
- Sensational terms such as “crisis”, “explosion” or “spiral” unless a defined source supports them.
- Treating correlation, arithmetic comparison or timing as proof of cause.
- Burying essential caveats in footnotes.

## Tables, charts and attribution

- Every analytical table in the article should have an associated visual or a documented reason why a chart would not improve understanding.
- The article should contain the strongest visual for each finding. Alternative formats for social media belong in the project package, not all in the reading flow.
- Pie charts are reserved for simple part-to-whole comparisons with few categories. A 100% stacked bar is generally preferred because it is easier to compare and adapt across screen sizes.
- Internal figure identifiers may remain in filenames and asset manifests, but should not appear in the visible chart header.
- The visible chart header uses the wordmark: **KENYA IN DATA • [TOPIC]**.
- Each visual must include two provenance lines:
  - `Data: [institution, report, table/figure, page]`
  - `Analysis and visualisation: Kenya in Data • kenyaindata.org`
- Captions repeat the essential source information because images and article text may circulate separately.
- Provide PNG, SVG and PDF exports, plus alt text and a machine-readable source record.

## Article page and project page

Each publication has two linked public surfaces:

- The **article page** contains the report narrative, selected tables, figures, glossary and references.
- The **project page** contains the complete dataset, data dictionary, metadata, all chart variants, downloadable assets, methodology, source ledger and reuse information.

The article should remain readable without visiting the project page. The project page should make every published numerical claim reproducible.

## Internal editorial notes

Reader-facing prose must not contain working comments, questions to collaborators or production instructions. Record them in the release checklist or as hidden Markdown comments. Resolve or remove all such notes before publication.

## Quality test before release

A publication is editorially ready when a reviewer can answer “yes” to each question:

1. Does the title state the subject accurately?
2. Are the main findings visible before the detailed analysis?
3. Can a non-specialist understand every key term?
4. Does every important number include a date, unit and denominator where relevant?
5. Is every table integrated with the prose and a suitable visual?
6. Are fact, calculation, interpretation and limitation clearly distinguishable?
7. Are primary sources cited close to the claims and on the figures?
8. Are working comments absent from the public body?
9. Can the project page reproduce every published figure and number?
10. Does the conclusion follow from the evidence without exaggeration?
